# Murugan and the fruit of wisdom: sources

Read 20 Nov 2026 (through a page reader; Wikipedia's API refused direct requests).

1. Wikipedia, "Dhandayuthapani Swamy Temple" (Palani Murugan Temple), Mythology section, cited there to
   M. Bhoothalingam, *Temples of India: Myths and Legends* (2016), pp. 48-52.
   - Sage Narada brings Shiva the gnana-palam, the fruit of knowledge, at Kailasa.
   - Shiva wants to divide it between his sons; Narada says it cannot be divided.
   - It will go to whoever first goes round the world three times.
   - Murugan sets off on his peacock; Ganesha reasons that the world is no more than his parents, goes round
     Shiva and Shakti, and wins the fruit.
   - Murugan comes back, is upset that his effort was in vain, feels cheated, leaves Kailasa and lives on the
     Palani hills as a hermit, having given up his belongings.
   - At Palani he is a young recluse, head shaven, in a loincloth, holding only a staff (dhandam):
     Dhandapani. Devotees shave their heads there in imitation of this form.
   - The panchamritam is believed to have been made first by Ganesha to soothe Muruga after the contest.
   - The Sangam poem Tirumurugarruppadai calls the place Thiru Aavinankudi.
2. Wikipedia, "Palani".
   - "Shiva awarded the fruit to Ganesha", pleased with his discernment.
   - Murugan "discarded all his robes and ornaments" and meditated on the hill.
   - The name: palam (fruit) + nee (you), "a reference to poet Avvaiyar's song praising Muruga which forms part of
     the legend" (uncited there); the page also gives another explanation of the name.
3. Wikipedia, "Kartikeya" (read in summary): the same story, attributed to the Kanda Puranam tradition; the fruit
   is usually taken to be a mango; Kamil Zvelebil reads it as a symbol: Murugan himself is the fruit of wisdom
   for his devotees.

## How the video tells it

- It is a scripture and temple story, told as a story: "grandmothers across Tamil Nadu tell", "as the story is
  told".
- Who said "Pazham nee" differs: some tellings give it to his parents, others to the poet Avvaiyar. The script
  says both. The name of Palani coming from it is "many people say".
- Not used: the Idumban story (another Palani legend, for a later video), the Panchamirtham recipe beyond
  "fruits, honey and jaggery".
